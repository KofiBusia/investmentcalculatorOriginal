The Big Idea: Three Questions, Three Statements

Before any numbers — understand why three separate documents exist at all. Every company answers three different questions, and each question needs its own statement:

  • "Did I make a profit?" → the Income Statement. Example: you sold GHS 500 of bread. Flour, rent and wages cost GHS 350. You made GHS 150 profit.
  • "Do I have the money?" → the Cash Flow Statement. But GHS 200 of that bread went to a customer on credit — only GHS 300 came in, and you spent GHS 350. Cash actually FELL by GHS 50.
  • "What do I own and owe?" → the Balance Sheet. You own an oven, GHS 200 owed to you, and less cash than last month. You owe the flour supplier GHS 80.
The lesson: the shop made a GHS 150 profit and lost GHS 50 of cash in the same month. Both statements are correct — they are answering different questions. This is the whole reason three statements exist, and the reason companies with beautiful profits sometimes collapse without warning.

Read cash flow first (to check you can believe the numbers), then income statement (to understand the business), then balance sheet (to see the running total of both). That order — not the order they're printed in — is how analysts actually read accounts.

1. The Income Statement

Also called the Statement of Comprehensive Income, Profit & Loss, or P&L. It covers a period — 1 January to 31 December 2025 — and answers: did MTN make a profit, and where did every cedi of revenue go?

MTN FY2025 in one line: Revenue GHS 24.43BN → Profit after tax GHS 7.84BN.

Read it as a funnel: one number (revenue) enters at the top, costs are subtracted in layers, and what survives at the bottom is profit.

Revenue — the top line

Revenue is the value of goods and services MTN delivered to customers during 2025, not what was paid. Accounting recognises revenue when the service is provided, not when money arrives. A customer who used GHS 50 of data in December but pays in January still counts as December revenue. Prepaid airtime bought in December but not yet used is not revenue — it sits as a "contract liability" until the customer actually uses it.

Other income

GHS 13.18 million in FY2025 — money earned outside the main business (prize money from MTN Group's "Ambition 2025" challenge, plus gaming-platform testing support). The Company-only figure (GHS 113.47M) is far higher than the Group figure because Scancom PLC charges its subsidiary MobileMoney Limited for admin support, which cancels out on consolidation.

Direct costs

The cost of physically delivering a call — these scale with usage: direct network operating costs GHS 2,290,857k (transmission, network, leased lines, spectrum & regulatory fees), devices cost GHS 78,606k, interconnect & roaming cost GHS 570,792k. Interconnect costs fell 17.8% even as revenue grew 36.1% — Ghanaians increasingly use WhatsApp/data instead of cross-network calls, so MTN pays less but also earns less interconnect revenue. Direct costs fell from 16.4% of revenue (FY2024) to 12.0% (FY2025).

Operating expenses

Employee expenses (GHS 1,061,001k, only 4.3% of revenue — telecoms aren't labour-intensive), selling/distribution/marketing (GHS 3,411,149k — the biggest cost in the company, more than 3x the wage bill, because every MoMo agent takes a commission on every transaction), and other operating expenses (GHS 2,340,752k — Group management fees, professional fees, insurance, admin).

EBITDA

Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation = GHS 14,690,333k, a 60.1% margin (up from 57.1% in 2024). EBITDA lets you compare two telecoms' operating performance without debt levels or tax positions getting in the way. But EBITDA is not cash and not profit — it ignores that MTN must spend billions a year replacing and expanding towers. Management's own guidance ("mid-to-upper fifties") suggests 60.1% may not repeat.

Depreciation, amortisation & EBIT

The tower example: MTN buys a tower for GHS 100M that will serve customers for 10 years. Charging the whole GHS 100M in year one would collapse that year's profit and make the next nine years look artificially wonderful. So accounting spreads the cost — GHS 10M a year for 10 years. That's depreciation. Amortisation is the same idea for things you can't touch — spectrum licences and software.

Memorise this: depreciation is a cost, but it is not a payment. MTN's bank balance did not fall by GHS 2.32BN because of depreciation — the cash left years ago when the tower was bought.

EBIT (Operating Profit) = EBITDA − D&A = GHS 11,960,724k, a 49.0% margin. The 11.1 percentage-point gap between the EBITDA margin (60.1%) and EBIT margin (49.0%) IS the cost of the network wearing out. Analysts still quote EBITDA because depreciation involves judgement (useful life, method) that can differ between identical companies — EBITDA strips that judgement out.

Finance income, finance costs & tax

Finance income GHS 450,066k (interest earned on MTN's cash); finance costs GHS 1,098,785k (mostly IFRS 16 lease "interest" plus FX losses on dollar-priced equipment — MTN has zero bank borrowings). Net finance cost: GHS 648,719k. Profit before tax: GHS 11,312,005k.

Ghana taxes MTN twice on the same profit: the Growth & Sustainability Levy (5% of PBT = GHS 565,600k) and income tax (25% + adjustments = GHS 2,906,475k). Total taxes and levies: GHS 3,472,075k — an effective rate of 30.7% of PBT.

Profit after tax & EPS

Profit after tax (PAT) = GHS 7,839,930k — up 55.9% on FY2024, a 32.1% net margin. PAT can only go two places: dividends (~GHS 4.53BN paid) or retained earnings (~GHS 3.31BN kept). EPS = GHS 7,839,930k ÷ 13.24BN shares = GHS 0.592. Against a July 2026 share price of GHS 6.44, that's a P/E of about 10.9x. But you haven't yet seen whether that profit is backed by real cash — that's the whole point of the Cash Flow Statement.

2. The Cash Flow Statement

"Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact." The income statement can be shaped by accounting estimates (how many years to depreciate a tower, how much to provide for bad debts). Cash is different — either the money is in the account on 31 December, or it isn't. There's no judgement available.

MTN FY2025 in one line: Profit GHS 7.84BN → Operating cash GHS 7.6BN → Bank balance up GHS 1.01BN.

This does not mean the income statement is dishonest — a business that only measured cash would look terrible in any year it invested, and wonderful in any year it stopped investing. Read cash flow first to check you can believe the income statement; then read the income statement to understand the business. MTN, like almost every listed company, builds this statement using the indirect method: start with profit, then reverse out everything that affected profit but not cash, and adjust for everything that affected cash but not profit.

Section 1: Operating Activities

Step 1 — reverse the non-cash items: start with profit before tax (GHS 11,312,005k), add back depreciation (+2,322,138), amortisation (+407,471) and net finance costs (+648,719). The subtotal lands exactly on EBITDA (14,690,333) — not a coincidence, since EBITDA is defined as earnings before precisely those items.

Step 2 — working capital: receivables rising means revenue was booked but cash wasn't collected (deduct); payables rising means a cost hit profit but cash stayed in the bank (add back); contract liabilities rising means cash came in before the service was delivered (add back — MTN sold GHS 18.26BN of prepaid airtime in 2025, an interest-free loan from customers that means growth funds MTN rather than starving it of cash, unlike most businesses).

Step 3 — tax actually paid: GHS 3,394,101k paid in cash vs GHS 2,906,475k charged on the income statement — a GHS 487,626k gap from timing, deferred tax, and prior-year GRA assessments (2014–2022 audits still open, "no reliable estimate" of exposure — the single largest unquantified risk in the accounts).

Result: Net cash from operating activities = GHS 7.6BN, +59.4% YoY. Cash conversion = OCF ÷ EBITDA = 7.6 ÷ 14.69 = 52% — strong for a heavily-taxed telecom.

For a mature company this is almost entirely outbound: dividends paid GHS 4,528,475k (net of 8% withholding tax — the training deck states the FY2025 declared dividend at GHS 6.4BN, +57.4% YoY); lease principal repayments; and borrowings, which went from GHS 70,443k (2024) to zero (2025) — MTN is now completely debt-free. MTN funded its entire capex programme and a large dividend from internally generated cash — no bank needed.

3. The Balance Sheet

Also called the Statement of Financial Position. Unlike the other two statements, it covers a moment, not a period — 31 December 2025. It answers: what does MTN own, what does it owe, and what is left for shareholders?

The equation that cannot be broken: Assets = Liabilities + Equity → GHS 62,391,057k = GHS 48,219,171k + GHS 14,171,886k. This isn't a rule someone invented — every cedi of value MTN controls was funded by a lender (liability) or an owner/retained profit (equity). If a balance sheet doesn't balance, someone made an error.

The MoMo float — read this before any ratio

GHS 38,392,517k of MTN's GHS 62,391,057k balance sheet is cash sitting in customers' MoMo wallets — held by MTN, not owned by it. There's a matching liability (obligations to e-money holders) of the exact same amount, so the net effect is zero: MTN is a custodian, not an owner, of this money.

Wrong (beginner) read: "MTN has GHS 62.4BN of assets — bigger than most Ghanaian banks." Right (analyst) read: "Strip the float — MTN's real balance sheet is about GHS 24.0BN of assets against GHS 9.83BN of liabilities." IFRS requires showing both sides because MTN legally controls the asset even though it must hand it back. This float is also why MobileMoney Limited (MMFL) was separated into its own licensed entity.

4. How All Three Connect — Six Hard Links

The three statements are not separate documents — they're three views of one set of events. Six links tie them together, and each is arithmetic that must hold.

  1. Profit → Retained income (IS → BS): Opening retained income (7,992,987) + PAT (7,839,930) − Dividends (4,528,475) = Closing retained income (11,304,442). The master link.
  2. Depreciation's three roles (IS → CF → BS): the same GHS 2,322,138 cuts profit on the income statement, is added back to cash on the cash flow statement, and reduces PPE's carrying value on the balance sheet.
  3. Capex → Assets → D&A (CF → BS → IS): GHS 6,414,306 of cash becomes towers (balance sheet), which become tomorrow's depreciation (income statement). The circle closes and restarts every year.
  4. Closing cash is an identity (CF = BS): GHS 4,298,477 must appear in both the cash flow statement's closing balance and the balance sheet's cash line — no tolerance, no exceptions. If they ever differ, someone made an error or is committing fraud.
  5. Tax charge vs tax paid (IS ≠ CF → BS): GHS 2,906,475 charged, GHS 3,394,101 paid; the GHS 487,626 difference parks on the balance sheet as a current tax asset.
  6. Working capital's home (IS ≠ CF → BS): every gap between revenue and cash sits somewhere on the balance sheet — receivables, contract liabilities, or payables. Nothing is ever lost; it just moves address.
Your method — how to read any company in ten minutes: (1) cash flow statement first — is operating cash positive and growing? (2) compare operating cash to profit; (3) compare capex to depreciation (MTN: 2.35x — still building); (4) balance sheet: debt and cash; (5) income statement last — margins and growth as confirmation, not the story; (6) read the notes (contingent liabilities, related parties); (7) pull five years of history, since one year is a snapshot of a machine mid-cycle.

Glossary

RevenueValue of services delivered to customers — not necessarily paid for yet.
EBITDAEarnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, Amortisation. Operating engine, not cash, not profit.
DepreciationSpreading the cost of a physical asset over its useful life. A cost, not a payment.
AmortisationThe same idea, applied to intangibles: licences, software.
EBIT / Operating profitEBITDA less D&A. The honest measure of operating performance.
PBT / PATProfit before tax / profit after tax (the bottom line, belongs to shareholders).
EPSPAT ÷ number of shares. MTN: GHS 0.592.
OCFOperating cash flow — money the business actually generated.
CapexCapital expenditure — cash spent buying long-lived assets.
FCFFree cash flow — OCF less capex. What is genuinely available to owners.
Working capitalThe gap between when business happens and when money moves.
ReceivablesMoney customers owe MTN for services already delivered.
Contract liabilityMoney customers paid MTN for services NOT yet delivered (unused airtime).
AccrualRecognising a transaction when it happens, not when cash moves — the basis of the income statement.
IFRS 16The rule that puts leased assets on the balance sheet as if bought with borrowed money.
Retained incomeAccumulated profits never paid out as dividends — the link between P&L and balance sheet.
Book value per shareTotal equity ÷ shares. MTN: GHS 1.07 against a GHS 6.44 market price.
